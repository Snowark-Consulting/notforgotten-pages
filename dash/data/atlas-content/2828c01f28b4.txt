# Website Validation Framework — Permanent

**Date:** 5 August 2026
**Author:** Atlas, with James Hardstaff
**Status:** Locked — Permanent Operating Standard

> Great positioning is not created by clever copy. It is discovered through repeated customer conversations.

---

## 1. The Validation Rule

| Sample Size | What It Produces |
|---|---|
| **1 person** | Feedback. Interesting. Do not act on it. |
| **3 people** | A pattern. Worth investigating. |
| **5 people** | Evidence. Now you can make a decision. |

Positioning changes only when multiple independent business owners reach the same conclusion. Never after a single conversation. Never based on internal opinion.

---

## 2. The Four Categories

Every observation from every interview is classified into one of four categories.

### Category A — Recognition

The participant recognised themselves or their business in the page.

Examples:
- "That actually happens in my business."
- "I've definitely lost work this way."
- "The insurance one — that's exactly what happens."

**Why it matters:** Recognition means the insight is universal enough to land. If recognition is low, the problem description isn't specific enough.

### Category B — Confusion

The participant misunderstood something or couldn't explain what we do.

Examples:
- "So is it software, or a person?"
- "I wasn't sure if you were a consultancy."
- "What do you actually do?"

**Why it matters:** Confusion is evidence. Do not explain it away. Record it. If the same confusion appears across 3+ participants, the section causing it needs rewriting.

### Category C — Resistance

The participant understood the message but didn't believe it or didn't think it applied to them.

Examples:
- "That wouldn't work in my industry."
- "My quotes are different."
- "I don't think my customers would respond to that."

**Why it matters:** Resistance tells us where the belief chain breaks. It may be a trust issue (proof not strong enough), a relevance issue (problem not framed for their industry), or a credibility issue (claim too strong for the evidence).

### Category D — Breakthrough

The participant changed how they think. This is the strongest validation we can receive.

Examples:
- "I've never thought about it like that."
- "I assumed those quotes were gone."
- "I hadn't considered that before."
- "I wonder how much I've lost."

**Why it matters:** Breakthrough moments are the awakening. They are the entire point of the page. Every section should aim to produce at least one Category D observation. If no participant has a breakthrough, the awakening failed.

---

## 3. The Success Metric

The website is successful if it changes how people think.

Not if they compliment the design. Not if they say it looks professional. Not if they say the copy is good.

**Success = Category D observations outnumber everything else.**

| Pattern | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Multiple Category D observations across participants | The positioning is working. Proceed to copywriting. |
| Mostly Category A (recognition) but no breakthrough | The problem resonates but the awakening isn't landing. Fix the hero. |
| Multiple Category B (confusion) | The copy is unclear. Simplify. Remove jargon. Test again. |
| Multiple Category C (resistance) | The proof isn't strong enough or the problem doesn't feel universal. Adjust. |

After every interview, produce a one-page summary. Do not recommend changes after a single interview. Wait until patterns emerge.

## 5. Permanent Principle

Great positioning is not created by clever copy. It is discovered through repeated customer conversations.

- Protect the positioning.
- Let the market teach us.
- Evidence decides.
- Opinion does not.
