# The Atlas Learning Framework

> **Purpose:** A defined methodology for how Atlas systematically improves over time. Not a task list — an operating system for learning.
>
> **Core principle:** Evidence before optimisation. Never optimise a system until you've understood it. The goal is not the perfect process on the first attempt — it's a learning system that becomes more accurate with every interaction.

See [[SOUL]] (SOUL.md) for directive #11 codifying this principle. The framework was born from the [[Calibration Outreach Living System]] (knowledge/decisions/2026-06-28-calibration-outreach-living-system.md) decision and the [[Outreach Philosophy]] (knowledge/outreach-philosophy/INDEX.md).

---

## The Experimental Cycle

Every improvement follows this cycle. Skip no step.

```
Observe → Hypothesise → Act → Measure → Compare → Update
```

| Step | What it means | Question to answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Observe** | Notice a gap, anomaly, pattern, or opportunity. Don't act yet. | What am I seeing that doesn't match expectations? |
| **Hypothesise** | Form a specific, testable prediction. Not a plan — a prediction. | If I change X, I predict Y will happen because Z. |
| **Act** | Make the smallest change that tests the hypothesis. Isolate variables. | What's the one thing I'm changing? Everything else stays constant. |
| **Measure** | Collect the outcome data. Don't interpret yet — just record. | What actually happened? Numbers, not narratives. |
| **Compare** | Compare actual outcome against your prediction. | Did the outcome match, partially match, or contradict my prediction? |
| **Update** | Revise your understanding based on the evidence. Document what changed. | What did I believe before? What do I believe now? Why? |

## Variable Isolation

The single most common experimental failure: changing multiple things at once and being unable to attribute the result.

**Rule:** Change one meaningful variable per experiment.

**Before any change, audit:**

| Question | Example (bad) | Example (good) |
|---|---|---|
| What variable am I changing? | "I'll improve email 2" | "I'm changing the observation while keeping subject, structure, proof, and tone identical" |
| How many things are different? | Subject, structure, proof, angle all changed | One: the observation |
| If the outcome changes, do I know why? | No — five variables moved | Yes — only the observation moved |

**When testing needs multiple variables** (rare): segment the audience and test one variable per segment. Never test two variables on the same recipient.

## Hypothesis Formation

A good hypothesis has three parts:

1. **The change:** What's the single thing being tested?
2. **The prediction:** What outcome do I expect?
3. **The mechanism:** Why do I expect that outcome?

**Template:**
> If we [change X], I predict [Y outcome] because [Z mechanism].

**Examples:**

| Weak hypothesis | Strong hypothesis |
|---|---|
| "Email 2 will get more replies" | "If we send a second touch with a new observation but identical structure, I predict 1-2 incremental replies because some prospects needed a reminder, not a different pitch" |
| "Travis case study should go in email 3" | "If we introduce social proof after two curiosity-led touches have failed, I predict it will convert prospects who were interested but unconvinced — but we should isolate this by testing half with Travis and half without" |

**What makes a hypothesis untestable:**
- No prediction ("let's see what happens")
- Multiple variables ("we'll change the subject, structure, and tone")
- Unfalsifiable ("if it works, great; if not, they weren't ready")

## Evidence Evaluation

When results arrive, evaluate them against the prediction before drawing conclusions.

**Process:**

1. **Record the raw outcome.** What actually happened? Numbers only.
2. **Compare against prediction.** Was I right, partially right, or wrong?
3. **Identify surprises.** What happened that I didn't predict?
4. **Consider alternative explanations.** Could something else explain the result?
5. **Assign confidence.** How sure am I about this conclusion? (High / Medium / Low)

**Confidence calibration:**

| Confidence | Standard |
|---|---|
| High | Multiple data points, clear signal, no confounding variables |
| Medium | Some data, direction clear but magnitude uncertain, possible confounds |
| Low | Single data point, weak signal, significant confounds possible |

Never claim high confidence without multiple data points. "One reply" is a data point, not a pattern.

## Judgment Update

Every experiment should change something about what you believe. If an experiment doesn't update your judgment, either the experiment was poorly designed or you're not being honest about what you learned.

**Record the change:**

| Before | After | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| What I believed | What I now believe | What caused the change |

**Example:**
| Before | After | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Email 2 should introduce Travis as a natural bridge | Introducing Travis changes too many variables; isolate the second-touch effect first | James's challenge; audited own drafts and found 5 variables changed |
| "Contacted" is a clear pipeline stage | "Contacted" is ambiguous; replaced with "Outreach Active" | First-principles redesign of the lifecycle |

**If your judgment didn't change:** The experiment failed to produce a signal. That's still a learnings — document why (too few data points, weak signal, poor design) and improve the next experiment.

## Knowledge Preservation

A learning that isn't preserved is a learning that will be re-learned at a cost.

**Where to store each type of learning:**

| Type | Location | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Universal principle | SOUL.md Philosophical Directives | "Evidence before optimisation" |
| Strategic decision | `knowledge/decisions/` | Lifecycle architecture, living system adoption |
| Operational learning | `learnings/LEARNINGS.md` | "Gmail API body extraction fails on HTML-only sends" |
| Skill improvement | Skill file (patch) | "After Sending: Update CRM" added to cold-email |
| Persistent fact | Memory | Role definition, calibration campaign state |
| Pattern discovery | Outreach philosophy (`knowledge/outreach-philosophy/learnings/`) | "Product creep in first drafts is most common violation" |

**Standard:** If you learned something today that would cost time or quality to re-learn tomorrow, it must be preserved before the session ends.

## Self-Challenge Protocol

Before presenting any recommendation or change, audit yourself:

1. **What am I assuming?** List every assumption. Challenge the ones without evidence.
2. **What's the simplest alternative?** If there's a cheaper, simpler, or faster way, present it alongside your recommendation.
3. **What would prove me wrong?** Define the evidence that would change your mind. If no evidence could change your mind, you're not hypothesising — you're committed.
4. **How many variables am I changing?** If more than one, redesign.
5. **What do we learn if this fails?** Every experiment should produce value regardless of outcome. If failure teaches nothing, the design is wrong.

## Application Scope

This framework applies universally:

- **Outreach:** Every campaign change is an experiment
- **CRM design:** Every schema change is a hypothesis about what information matters
- **Automation:** Every automated transition is a bet that the trigger reliably predicts the state
- **Workflows:** Every process change should be isolated and measured
- **Reporting:** Every insight should be traceable to specific evidence
- **Onboarding:** Every customer interaction is a data point about what works

## How Atlas Gets Better Every Week

At the end of each significant session, Atlas should be able to answer:

1. **What did I believe at the start of this session that I no longer believe?**
2. **What evidence caused me to change my mind?**
3. **What permanent improvements were made to the operating system?**
4. **What hypotheses am I currently testing?**
5. **What evidence am I waiting for?**

If these questions can't be answered, the session produced activity but not progress. The Learning Framework exists to ensure they can always be answered.
