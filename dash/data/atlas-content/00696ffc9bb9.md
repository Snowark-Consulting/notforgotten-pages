# 05 — Questions That Create Curiosity

> The right question is the difference between a deleted email and a reply. A bad question feels like a sales tactic. A good question feels like something the reader should already know — and the discomfort of not knowing is what makes them reply.

---

## What Makes A Question Good

| Quality | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Genuine** | You'd actually ask this in conversation. It's not a rhetorical setup for a pitch. |
| **Specific** | Connected to their actual business — services, scale, industry. Not generic. |
| **Open** | Can't be answered with yes/no. Requires thought. Invites elaboration. |
| **Gap-surfacing** | Makes them think "I should know this" — and they might not. |
| **Non-judgmental** | Doesn't imply they're doing something wrong. Just curious. |

---

## Question Types (with examples)

### Type 1: The Visibility Question

Surfaces the gap between sending a quote and knowing what happened to it.

| Good | Why it works |
|---|---|
| "Once a quote leaves your hands, how do you know what happens to it? Not whether they said yes or no. Whether they saw it at all." | Specific. Non-obvious. Most people only track yes/no. This asks about the in-between. |
| "When you send a quote on a Tuesday, how do you know on Friday whether it's been opened?" | Time-bound. Specific. Makes the gap tangible. |
| "After you've quoted 20 jobs in a month, how do you know which ones are still in play?" | Volume + uncertainty. Two dimensions of the gap. |

### Type 2: The Process Question

Surfaces the gap between intention ("I should follow up") and action ("did I?").

| Good | Why it works |
|---|---|
| "What's your process for following up on quotes — or does it depend on how busy the week is?" | Most people's process IS "depends how busy I am." The question lets them admit it without judgment. |
| "Who handles follow-up in your business — or is it one of those things that everyone assumes someone else is doing?" | Surfaces the ownership gap. Follow-up often has no owner. |
| "How do you decide which quotes to follow up on first?" | Most people don't prioritise. The question reveals the absence of a system. |

### Type 3: The Volume Question

Surfaces the gap between how many quotes they send and how many they track.

| Good | Why it works |
|---|---|
| "Roughly how many quotes do you send in a typical month?" | Simple. Quantifiable. If they don't know, that's the gap. If they do know, it opens a specific conversation. |
| "With the range of services you cover, I imagine you're quoting everything from small residential to large commercial. Does the follow-up approach change, or is it the same across the board?" | Specific to their business. Acknowledges complexity. Invites process thinking. |

### Type 4: The Reflection Question

Surfaces the gap between what they think is happening and what's actually happening.

| Good | Why it works |
|---|---|
| "If you had to guess, what percentage of your quotes go completely unanswered — not a yes, not a no, just silence?" | Quantifiable. Most people have never calculated this. The number is usually higher than they think. |
| "When was the last time you quoted a job and never heard back — and you're still not sure why?" | Specific. Memorable. Every business owner has this story. |

---

## Question Patterns To Avoid

| Bad pattern | Why it's bad | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Have you ever considered...?" | Leading. The answer is always no (or they'd already be doing it). | Ask about their current process instead. Let them discover the gap. |
| "Did you know that...?" | The email is now a lecture. They're being told, not asked. | Turn the statistic into a question. "What percentage of your quotes would you guess go unanswered?" |
| "Are you interested in...?" | Binary. Easy to say no. Also, it's a sales question. | Ask about their experience, not their interest. Interest follows experience. |
| "Wouldn't it be great if...?" | Rhetorical. Manipulative. The answer is forced. | Don't. Just don't. |
| Multiple questions in one email | Interrogation. Overwhelming. They'll answer none. | One question per email. One. |

---

## The Question Test

After writing, ask yourself:

1. **Could they answer this without thinking?** If yes, the question is too easy. They'll answer and move on without engaging.
2. **Does the question contain the answer we want?** If yes, it's a leading question. Rewrite.
3. **Is the question genuinely about them, or is it a setup for our product?** If it's a setup, they'll feel it. Rewrite.
4. **If I received this question from a stranger, would I want to answer it?** If no, why would they?

## Industry-Specific Question Templates

These are starting points, not copy-paste. Every question must be adapted to the specific business.

### Painting / Trades

- "With five crews across Adelaide, how do you keep track of which quotes are still in play?"
- "You've been painting Adelaide homes since 2008. After a quote goes out, what's your process for staying in touch?"
- "Roughly how many quotes do you send in a busy month — and how many of those do you personally follow up on?"

### Commercial Cleaning

- "ACS covers medical, dental, schools, offices, strata. That's a lot of different quotes going out each month. Once a quote leaves your hands, how do you know what happens to it?"
- "With contracts ranging from small offices to large medical facilities, does your follow-up approach change depending on the size of the job?"
- "How do you prioritise which quotes to follow up on when you're managing multiple sites?"

### Marketing Agencies

- "I noticed you work across brand strategy, digital, and content. With so many different types of proposals going out, how do you track which ones are still alive?"
- "In agency life, it's easy for proposals to get lost between client work and new business. What's your system for making sure nothing falls through the cracks?"
- "When you send a proposal, how do you know whether the client has actually reviewed it — or if it's sitting in an inbox somewhere?"

---

## The Meta-Question

Every question should implicitly ask: **"Do you have certainty about this part of your business, or is there a gap you haven't noticed?"**

If the question doesn't point toward that uncertainty, it's probably not the right question.
