# 01 — Core Outreach Principles

> These are not guidelines. They are the philosophy. If an email violates any of these, it doesn't ship. Challenge James if necessary — even if he wrote it.

---

## Principle 1: Build a relationship before selling

**What it means:** The first email earns attention. The second earns trust. The third earns curiosity. Selling only happens after all three. You cannot build a relationship in one email. You cannot diagnose a business from three sentences. The first email's job is singular: earn the right to send a second one.

**What this looks like in practice:**
- First email: one observation, one question, one reason. Zero product.
- Second email (after reply): acknowledge their response, go deeper on their specific situation, still zero product.
- Third email: only now, if they've engaged meaningfully, can the product appear — and even then, as a possibility, not a pitch.

**Violation example:** "We help service businesses recover lost revenue through automated quote follow-up. Would you be interested in a demo?" — This sells before building anything. The relationship doesn't exist yet. Delete and rewrite.

**Correct approach:** "ACS covers medical, dental, schools, offices, strata. That's a lot of different quotes going out each month. I'm curious — once a quote leaves your hands, how do you know what happens to it? Reason I ask: I've been working with service businesses on exactly this."

The product isn't mentioned. The relationship begins with curiosity, not commerce.

---

## Principle 2: Diagnose before demonstrating

**What it means:** Don't show the solution until the prospect recognises they have the problem. A doctor doesn't prescribe medicine before asking where it hurts. We don't pitch follow-up automation before the prospect has said "I don't actually know what happens to my quotes."

**What this looks like in practice:**
- The first email asks a diagnostic question — one that surfaces a gap
- The prospect's reply reveals whether they recognise the problem
- Only after the prospect has articulated the gap (in their own words) do we demonstrate anything

**Violation example:** "Did you know businesses lose 30% of revenue through poor quote follow-up? Our platform solves this." — This diagnoses AND demonstrates in the same sentence. The prospect hasn't had time to feel the problem. Delete and rewrite.

**Correct approach:** The ACS email doesn't mention lost revenue, follow-up rates, or any statistic. It asks a question that lets the prospect discover the gap themselves.

---

## Principle 3: Help the customer discover the gap themselves

**What it means:** People believe what they discover more than what they're told. The most powerful moment in our outreach is when a business owner thinks "I should know the answer to this, and I don't." That moment can't be manufactured. It has to be discovered.

**What this looks like in practice:**
- Questions that are genuine, not leading
- No statistics or "did you know" facts — those tell, not discover
- The gap is surfaced by absence: they can't answer the question, and that bothers them

**Violation example:** "Most businesses don't follow up on their quotes. You're probably losing thousands without realising it." — This tells them they have a problem. It doesn't help them discover it. It also assumes. Delete and rewrite.

**Correct approach:** "Once a quote leaves your hands, how do you know what happens to it? Not whether they said yes or no. Whether they saw it at all." — This doesn't tell them they're losing anything. It asks a question they may not have considered. If they haven't, the gap opens naturally.

---

## Principle 4: Never manipulate

**What it means:** No false scarcity. No manufactured urgency. No psychological tricks that exploit cognitive biases. If the only way to sell the product is through manipulation, the product isn't good enough. Fix the product — don't manipulate the customer.

**What this looks like in practice:**
- No "limited time" offers that aren't actually limited
- No "only 3 spots remaining" when there aren't
- No "prices increasing next week" when they're not
- No designed FOMO, countdown timers, or artificial deadlines

**Violation example:** Any sentence containing "limited," "exclusive," "only a few," or "expires" when those conditions aren't genuinely true. False urgency is a lie. We don't lie.

**Correct approach:** The value proposition must stand on its own. If the product genuinely recovers $150K+ for a business, $199/month doesn't need urgency to sell. The outcome sells itself.

---

## Principle 5: Never create false urgency

**What it means:** A subset of Principle 4, called out separately because it's the most common manipulation in B2B SaaS. "Book now — spots filling fast." "Early bird pricing ends Friday." If these are true (a real conference, a real deadline), fine. If they're manufactured, they're dishonest.

**What this looks like in practice:**
- Real deadlines are fine: "Our onboarding batch starts next Monday. If you'd like to be included, let me know by Friday."
- Manufactured deadlines are not: "I can only take on 3 more clients this month" when there's no actual capacity limit.

**Violation example:** "We're offering this at $199 for a limited time. Prices increase next month." — Unless this is genuinely true and the increase is already scheduled, it's manipulation. Don't do it.

**Correct approach:** "Our pricing is $199/month. We keep it there because we believe the product should earn the right to charge more." — Honest about the price and honest about why.

---

## Principle 6: Never oversell

**What it means:** One email, one job. The first email makes them think. It doesn't also pitch, prove, and close. Overselling is the fastest way to sound like everyone else in their inbox. Restraint is differentiation.

**What this looks like in practice:**
- One observation about their business
- One genuine question
- One sentence establishing why you're asking
- Nothing else

**Violation example:** A first email that includes: the problem statement, a statistic, the solution, the case study, and a call to action. That's five emails crammed into one. The prospect reads none of them.

**Correct approach:** The ACS email is 87 words. One observation. One question. One reason. 15 seconds to read. Nothing more.

---

## Principle 7: Earn the right to ask for the next conversation

**What it means:** Every email must justify why the next one deserves to be read. If you haven't provided value — a genuine insight, a useful question, a moment of reflection — you haven't earned the follow-up. Permission, not persistence.

**What this looks like in practice:**
- The "ask" in the first email is a reply — the smallest possible commitment
- The ask is only made after delivering something of value (the question itself is the value)
- If they don't reply, you haven't earned another email. Pause. Re-evaluate. Don't chase.

**Violation example:** "I'll follow up next week if I don't hear back." — This presumes permission that hasn't been granted. Also, it's a threat dressed as courtesy.

**Correct approach:** No mention of follow-up. No "I'll circle back." The email stands alone. If they reply, you've earned the next one. If they don't, the email wasn't good enough — fix the email, don't chase the prospect.

---

## Principle 8: Sell certainty, not software

**What it means:** The product isn't quote follow-up automation. The product is confidence that no opportunity is forgotten. Every email should make the prospect feel the absence of that certainty — the quiet anxiety of not knowing what happens to quotes once they leave your hands.

**What this looks like in practice:**
- The word "follow-up" doesn't appear in the first email
- The word "automation" never appears
- The email doesn't describe what the product does — it describes what the prospect is missing
- The feeling the email creates is: "I should know this. Why don't I know this?"

**Violation example:** "We automate quote follow-up so you never miss an opportunity." — This sells software. Features. Functions. The prospect doesn't care about automation. They care about the opportunity they just remembered they might be missing.

**Correct approach:** "Once a quote leaves your hands, how do you know what happens to it?" — This sells the absence of certainty. The prospect fills in the gap themselves: "I don't know. And that's a problem."

---

## The Meta-Principle

**Never optimise for opens. Never optimise for clicks. Never optimise for vanity metrics. Optimise for genuine conversations.**

A clever subject line might get an open. A catchy question might get a click. But a genuine, thoughtful, researched question gets a reply — from someone who's actually curious about their own business. That's the only metric that matters.

---

## The Test

After writing any outreach email, read it back and ask:

**"If I were the business owner receiving this, would I feel like someone genuinely understood my business, or would I feel like I was being sold to?"**

If it's the second, rewrite.
