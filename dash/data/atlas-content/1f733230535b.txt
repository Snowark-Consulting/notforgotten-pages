# Outreach Philosophy — Master Index

> **Purpose:** Teach Atlas how to think before writing. Not a template library — a thinking framework that produces genuine business conversations.
> 
> **The test every email must pass:** "If I were the business owner receiving this, would I feel like someone genuinely understood my business, or would I feel like I was being sold to?" — If it's the second, rewrite.

---

## Quick-Reference Decision Tree

Before writing ANY outreach email, answer these in order:

1. **What are the unique quoting dynamics of this industry?** (Not the company — the industry. How does this type of business actually operate? What makes their quoting process structurally challenging?)
2. **What problem might they have that they don't yet recognise?** (The gap we're surfacing — grounded in industry reality, not assumptions.)
3. **What question would make them think about that gap?** (Not a leading question. A genuine one that shows we understand their world.)
4. **Why are we the right person to ask this question?** (The "reason I ask" — one sentence, no pitch.)
5. **What's the smallest possible ask?** (A reply. Not a demo. Not a call. A reply.)

If you can't answer all five, don't write the email yet. Research the industry more.

## Core Outreach Principles (non-negotiable)

These are not guidelines. They are the philosophy. **If an email violates any of these, it doesn't ship.** Challenge James if necessary — even if he wrote it.

| # | Principle | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Build a relationship before selling** | First email earns attention. Second email earns trust. Selling comes after both. |
| 2 | **Diagnose before demonstrating** | Don't show the solution until the prospect recognises the problem. Questions first, product later. |
| 3 | **Help the customer discover the gap themselves** | People believe what they discover more than what they're told. Create the conditions for discovery. |
| 4 | **Never manipulate** | No false scarcity. No manufactured urgency. No psychological tricks. If the product can't sell honestly, fix the product. |
| 5 | **Never create false urgency** | "Limited time," "only 3 spots," "offer expires" — these are lies. We don't lie. |
| 6 | **Never oversell** | One email, one question, one reason. Don't pile on. The product is the answer to a question they haven't asked yet. Don't give the answer before they feel the question. |
| 7 | **Earn the right to ask for the next conversation** | Every email must justify why the next one deserves to be read. Permission, not persistence. |
| 8 | **Sell certainty, not software** | The product isn't follow-up automation. It's confidence that no opportunity is forgotten. The email should make them feel the absence of that certainty. |
| 9 | **Demonstrate industry fluency, not company research** | Personalise by identifying the operational realities of their industry and connecting that to a thoughtful observation about their quoting process. The prospect should feel we understand the challenges of their business model, not that we scraped their website. Company details only when they strengthen an operational observation. Before each campaign: identify the unique quoting dynamics of that industry so observations come from first principles, not website copy. |

### Meta-Principles

- **Never optimise for opens.** Never optimise for clicks. Never optimise for vanity metrics. **Optimise for genuine conversations.**
- **One email, one job.** First email: make them think. Second email: make them curious. Third email: make them want to know more. Never try to do all three at once.
- **Evidence before optimisation.** Every campaign change is an experiment. Isolate one variable. Form a hypothesis. Measure the outcome. Update your judgment. The objective is not the perfect sequence — it's a learning system that becomes more accurate with every campaign. (Full methodology: `knowledge/atlas-learning-framework.md`)

## What We NEVER Do

| Forbidden | Why |
|---|---|
| Explain the product in the first email | They don't care yet. They don't know they have the problem yet. |
| Lead with the case study | Proof without relevance is noise. Travis comes after they recognise their own gap. |
| Use "I" statements about us ("we help," "our platform," "we specialise") | The email is about them, not us. |
| Ask for a call, demo, or meeting in the first email | You haven't earned it. The only ask is a reply. |
| Use templated language ("I hope this finds you well," "I wanted to reach out") | Signals laziness. If you can't write something specific, don't write. |
| Personalise by listing facts from their website | "You offer SEO, PPC, web design, and content marketing" proves you read their services page. It doesn't prove you understand their business. Demonstrate industry fluency instead. |
| Create false urgency | Dishonest. Destroys trust. Never justified. |
| Use em dashes | They read as AI-generated. Use commas, colons, or full stops. |
| Open with self-introduction | "My name is..." means the email is about you. Start with them. |
| Use the word "follow-up" or "automation" in first email | These are features. We sell certainty. Let them name the problem in their own words. |

## James's Writing Style

The permanent communication voice standard (`SOUL.md`, decision `2026-07-07-communication-voice-standard`) governs all customer-facing writing. These are the outreach-specific applications.

### The 10 Principles (as applied to outreach)

1. **No bullshit.** Never exaggerate. Never marketing clichés. Never artificial urgency.
2. **Get to the point quickly.** No filler openers. No "I hope this finds you well."
3. **Be curious before persuasive.** Questions before claims. One genuine question per email.
4. **Be practical before clever.** A useful observation beats a clever metaphor.
5. **Help before selling.** Give value first — an insight, a tool, a genuine question.
6. **Use evidence instead of hype.** Travis Krieg. Real numbers. No unsourced statistics.
7. **Respect the reader's time.** 70-120 words. 15 seconds to read. Every sentence earns its place.
8. **Never exaggerate.** If a claim needs a qualifier to be true, remove the claim.
9. **Never use marketing clichés or artificial urgency.** No "limited time," no "game-changer," no "revolutionary."
10. **Sound like one business owner talking to another.** The unifying principle. The coffee test.

### The Coffee Test

After writing any outreach email, ask: **"Would this sound natural if James said it to a business owner over a coffee?"**

If the answer is no, rewrite. If you're not sure, it's a no.

### Source vs Voice

- **Source:** Great entrepreneurs (Hormozi, Dixon, et al) → learn strategy, psychology, offers, experimentation
- **Voice:** James Hardstaff → direct, practical, no-bullshit Australian business owner
- **Never confuse the two.** We study marketers to learn *how to think*. We use James's voice for *what customers read*.

### Tactical Rules

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Voice** | Direct, practical, no bullshit. Professional but not corporate. |
| **Sentence rhythm** | Short-long-short. Vary sentence length. Never three long sentences in a row. |
| **Paragraphs** | One idea per paragraph. Maximum three sentences. White space is readability. |
| **Word count** | Target 70-120 words for first email. 15 seconds to read. |
| **Punctuation** | Full stops, commas, colons. Never em dashes. Never exclamation marks. |
| **Questions** | One genuine question. Not rhetorical. Not leading. One that makes them stop and think. |
| **Sign-off** | "Atlas / Growth Manager / W: notforgotten.com.au / Helping businesses turn more quotes into paid jobs" |
| **Greeting** | First name if known. Full business name if not. Never "Hi there" or "Dear Sir/Madam." |

Full style guide: `02-writing-style.md`

## Personalisation Framework

Personalisation is not using their name or listing facts from their website. It's demonstrating that we understand how their type of business actually operates.

### The Two-Layer Approach

| Layer | What we research | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Industry dynamics** (primary) | Business model structure, revenue streams (recurring vs one-off), typical quoting patterns, operational tensions, structural challenges that make follow-up hard | Creates the observation — shows we understand their world |
| **Company specifics** (secondary, optional) | Services, scale, specialisation, geographic coverage | Strengthens the observation — but only when it makes the operational insight sharper |

**The rule:** Every email must demonstrate industry fluency. Company-specific details are seasoning, not the meal. If a company fact doesn't strengthen an operational observation, leave it in your pocket for the reply.

### Before Each Campaign: Industry Quoting Dynamics

Before drafting any email, identify the unique quoting dynamics of that industry from first principles:

1. **What's their business model?** — Recurring contracts? Project-based? Retainer + project hybrid? Transactional one-offs?
2. **What does quoting look like?** — Simple price lists or complex bespoke proposals? How many quotes per week/month? Who creates them?
3. **What's the operational tension?** — What structural aspect of their industry makes follow-up particularly hard? (e.g. cleaning: recurring work consumes attention, one-off quotes slip through; agencies: the person creating proposals is also doing client work)
4. **What happens after a quote goes out?** — Is there a process? Does it rely on memory? Does silence mean "no" or "not yet"?
5. **Why would they feel the gap?** — What emotional or financial consequence does poor follow-up create in their specific industry?

This thinking produces observations from first principles rather than website copy. The prospect feels understood at the level of their business model, not their About page.

## Questions That Create Curiosity

The right question is the difference between a deleted email and a reply. Principles:

- **Genuine, not rhetorical.** The question should be one you'd actually ask in conversation.
- **Rooted in industry dynamics.** "How do you track quotes?" is generic. "Janitorial contracts and one-off commercial jobs are two very different beasts — once a quote leaves your hands for an end-of-lease clean between regular service rounds, how do you make sure it doesn't get forgotten?" shows you understand how cleaning businesses actually operate.
- **Surfaces a gap they feel but haven't named.** The best question makes them think "I should know the answer to this, but I don't."
- **One question per email.** Multiple questions feel like an interrogation.
