## Industry Context

Commercial cleaning is a quote-heavy industry. Businesses quote across multiple sectors (medical, dental, education, commercial, strata), each with different requirements, pricing structures, and decision-makers. A single cleaning company might have 20-50 active quotes at any time, ranging from small office cleans to multi-year hospital contracts.

The follow-up challenge is compounded by:
- **Diverse decision-makers:** A dental practice owner decides differently from a strata committee
- **Long sales cycles:** Large contracts can take months from quote to decision
- **No natural follow-up owner:** In owner-operated cleaning businesses, the owner quotes AND cleans AND manages — follow-up is what happens "when there's time"

## Business Researched

**ACS Commercial Cleaning** — South Australian commercial cleaning company.

What we discovered:
- **Services:** Medical, dental, schools, offices, strata — broad commercial range
- **Industry coverage:** Five distinct sectors, each with different cleaning standards and quoting requirements
- **Digital presence:** Website exists, professional but not extensive
- **Scale indicators:** Multi-sector suggests established business, not a sole trader

What we could NOT find:
- Contact first name (hence business-name greeting, per Founding Decision R6)
- Team size
- Specific quote volume

## Why This Opening

We chose "ACS covers medical, dental, schools, offices, strata" as the opening because:

1. **It demonstrates research.** We actually looked at their business. This isn't a template.
2. **It's specific, not flattering.** We're not saying "you're great." We're saying "you cover these five sectors." Factual observation.
3. **It sets up the question naturally.** "That's a lot of different quotes" follows logically from listing five sectors. It doesn't feel forced.
4. **It implies complexity without stating it.** We don't say "your quoting must be complex." The reader infers it from the list.

---

## Why This Question

"Once a quote leaves your hands, how do you know what happens to it? Not whether they said yes or no. Whether they saw it at all."

We chose this question because:

1. **It's non-obvious.** Most people only track yes/no. The in-between — "did they even open it?" — is rarely considered.
2. **It's specific to quoting.** Not "how do you manage your business?" but "what happens after the quote leaves?"
3. **It creates a curiosity gap.** Most business owners can't answer this precisely. The discomfort of not knowing is the engine of the reply.
4. **It doesn't blame.** "How do you know" not "why don't you know." The question assumes competence and asks about process.
5. **The clarification ("not whether they said yes or no") prevents the easy answer.** Without it, they can say "I track yes/no in a spreadsheet." With it, they have to think about the gap.

---

## What Problem We Believe They May Have

**Hypothesis:** ACS likely sends quotes across five different sectors to different types of decision-makers. Their follow-up process — if it exists — is probably inconsistent across sectors. A medical centre quote might get followed up differently from a strata quote. There's likely no single system for tracking where every quote stands at any given moment.

The owner probably handles follow-up personally (common in owner-operated cleaning businesses), meaning it competes with everything else they do. Some quotes almost certainly slip through.

**How we'll know if we're right:** If they reply with some version of "we don't really have a system for that" or "it depends on the contract" or "I handle it myself when I can."

**How we'll know if we're wrong:** If they reply with "we have a CRM that tracks everything" or "our admin team handles all follow-up systematically."

## Core Principles Applied

| Principle | How it's applied |
|---|---|
| **Build a relationship before selling** | Zero product mention. The email exists to start a conversation, not to pitch. |
| **Diagnose before demonstrating** | The question is diagnostic. It surfaces whether they have a gap. We don't assume. |
| **Help the customer discover the gap** | "How do you know what happens to it?" — lets them discover they don't know. |
| **Never oversell** | 87 words. One observation. One question. One reason. Nothing more. |
| **Earn the right to ask** | The only "ask" is implicit in the question. No request for a call, demo, or meeting. |
| **Sell certainty, not software** | The email is about the absence of certainty — what happens after the quote leaves. |

## Emotional Response We Hope To Create

We want the reader to feel:

1. **Seen.** "Someone actually looked at my business. This isn't spam."
2. **Curious.** "That's a good question. I've never thought about that."
3. **Mildly unsettled.** "I don't actually know what happens to my quotes after I send them. Should I?"

The combination of seen + curious + unsettled = motivation to reply. Not because they want to buy something, but because they genuinely want to answer the question — and the act of not being able to answer it bothers them.

---

## Action We Hope They Take

**Primary success:** They reply. Any reply. "Good question — we track everything in a spreadsheet" or "Honestly, we don't really have a system" or "Interesting — what do you mean exactly?" — all are wins. The reply opens the conversation.

**Secondary success:** They don't reply but they think about the question. The seed is planted. A follow-up email in 2-3 weeks might find them more receptive.

**Not success:** They mark as spam, unsubscribe, or reply with "not interested." If this happens, the email wasn't right for them — or we misjudged the fit.

---

## Success Definition

- Reply received: ✅ Success (conversation opened)
- No reply after 2 weeks: ⚠️ Neutral (seed may be planted; follow-up appropriate)
- Negative response: ❌ Failure (learn from it and adjust)
- Positive reply leading to conversation: 🎯 Target outcome
