# Founding Decision: No Product Pitch in First Cold Email

**Slug:** `founding-no-pitch-in-first-email`
**Date:** June 2026
**Status:** Made
**Review Date:** 2026-10-01
**Type:** Founding Decision — immutable. If thinking changes, create a new decision referencing this one.

---

## The Decision

The first cold email to any prospect must not explain the product, mention Not Forgotten Systems by function, or make any kind of pitch. The only job of the first email is to make the reader think about their own follow-up process — to surface a gap they may not know they have. One genuine observation about their business, one thoughtful question, one sentence establishing why you're asking. Approximately 70-120 words. 15 seconds to read.

---

## The Problem

Cold outreach typically fails for one of two reasons: either it's ignored (too generic, too salesy, too long) or it converts the wrong kind of customer (someone who was already looking for a solution and would have found one anyway). Not Forgotten's real market isn't business owners actively searching for follow-up software — it's business owners who don't yet know they have a follow-up problem. They think they have a sales problem, a marketing problem, or a lead quality problem.

You can't convince someone they have a different problem in one email. But you can get them to think about it.

---

## Options Considered

| # | Option | Summary |
|---|---|---|
| A | Pitch the product immediately | "We automate quote follow-up and recover lost revenue." Clear, direct. Risk: ignored by 95% of recipients, attracts only people already looking. |
| B | Lead with proof (Travis case study) | "$177,555 recovered for an Adelaide painter. Want to know how?" Risk: impressive but irrelevant if they don't recognise their own gap. |
| C | Lead with questions, no pitch | Make them think about their own process. No product mention at all. Risk: too subtle, they don't know what we want. |
| D | Hybrid: question + light positioning | Ask a question, briefly mention "I work with service businesses on exactly this." Risk: line between positioning and pitching is blurry. |

---

## Why This Path

Option C was chosen, with a slight lean toward Option D (the "reason I ask" sentence provides just enough context to make the question feel motivated rather than random).

James's insight from the interview: "You can't build a relationship in one email. You can't diagnose someone's business from three sentences. The first email's job is to earn the next 15 seconds of attention."

The benchmark email (ACS Commercial Cleaning) scored 35/40: one observation ("ACS covers medical, dental, schools, offices, strata — that's a lot of different quotes"), one question ("once a quote leaves your hands, how do you know what happens to it?"), one reason ("I've been working with service businesses on exactly this"). 87 words. No product, no pitch, no company name in the body.

This approach also filters. If someone replies, they're genuinely curious about their own process. If they don't, they either don't have the problem or aren't ready to think about it — and no pitch would have changed that.

---

## Core Principles Applied

- **Help prospects discover their own problem** — Don't convince. Ask questions that reveal the gap. (Source: Philosophical Directive 2)
- **Lead with questions, not product** — The product is the answer to a question they haven't asked yet. The first job is to make them ask it. (Source: Philosophical Directive 2)
- **Certainty, not features** — The first email shouldn't mention automation, software, or follow-up systems. It should make the reader feel uncertain about something they assumed was fine. (Source: Philosophical Directive 1)
- **We're asking businesses to trust us with their reputation** — A bad first email burns a prospect permanently. A good one earns permission to continue. (Source: Philosophical Directive 9)

---

## Risks Identified

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Too subtle — prospects don't understand what we're offering | Medium | Medium | Second email (after reply) provides context. The first email's only conversion metric is "did they reply?" |
| Lower reply volume than a direct pitch | Medium | Low | Reply quality matters more than quantity. One genuine conversation beats ten "tell me more" auto-replies. |
| Some industries want directness, not questions | Low | Low | Test per vertical. If certain industries (e.g., marketing agencies) respond better to direct approach, adapt. |
| Hard to measure — "made them think" isn't trackable | High | Low | Track reply rates, reply quality (subjective score), and eventual conversion. Not everything that matters is measurable in the first email. |

---

## Expected Outcome

Reply rates meet or exceed cold outreach benchmarks (5-10%). More importantly, the quality of replies is higher: prospects articulate their own follow-up gap, ask informed questions, and enter the conversation as curious participants rather than passive recipients of a pitch.

---

## Success Criteria

- [ ] Reply rate at or above 5% across first 100 outreach emails
- [ ] At least 50% of replies demonstrate genuine curiosity about their own process (not just "tell me more" or "unsubscribe")
- [ ] Zero replies complaining about vagueness or confusion about who we are
- [ ] At least 3 prospects articulate their own follow-up gap without being told what it is
- [ ] 15-second readability maintained — no first email exceeds 120 words

## Atlas's Challenge (Pre-Decision)

> The challenge came during the first outreach draft for ACS Commercial Cleaning. The initial version was overly explanatory — it described the product and pitched the solution. I flagged that it violated the "no pitch" principle James had articulated. James agreed and the email was rewritten. The final version was 87 words with zero product mention. The challenge process itself validated the decision: even with the principle established, the instinct to explain is strong. Without active pushback, the principle would drift.

**James's response:** Accepted. The rewritten email scored 35/40 and became the benchmark template for all future outreach.
