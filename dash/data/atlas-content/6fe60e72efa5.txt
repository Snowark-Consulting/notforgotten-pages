# Founding Decision: Tiered Trust — Admin Automation vs. Draft-and-Ask

**Slug:** `founding-tiered-trust-automation`
**Date:** June 2026
**Status:** Made
**Review Date:** 2026-12-01
**Type:** Founding Decision — immutable. If thinking changes, create a new decision referencing this one.

---

## The Decision

Atlas's autonomy is divided into two tiers. **Tier 1 (Admin Automation):** Pre-approved. Record creation, data linking, status updates, internal logging, dashboard updates. These are mechanical, reversible, and low-risk. Atlas can execute without asking. **Tier 2 (Draft-and-Ask):** Customer-facing decisions require James's approval before execution. Personalised emails, negotiation responses, complaint handling, interpreting ambiguous customer intent — draft, present, wait for explicit confirmation. Trust is earned in layers. Nothing customer-visible ships without review until explicitly promoted.

---

## The Problem

Automation is the product — but automation without guardrails is reputation risk. A system that sends the wrong email to the wrong prospect at the wrong time doesn't just waste an opportunity; it damages someone else's business reputation. Not Forgotten asks businesses to trust us with their customer communications. If Atlas makes a mistake that damages a client's customer relationship, we've broken the core promise.

At the same time, if every action requires approval, Atlas isn't automation — it's a slow assistant. The business can't scale if James is reviewing every Airtable update and every status change. There needs to be a line. Where is it?

---

## Options Considered

| # | Option | Summary |
|---|---|---|
| A | Full autonomy — Atlas does everything | Maximum speed, maximum risk. One bad email damages reputation permanently. |
| B | Zero autonomy — James approves everything | Maximum safety, minimum speed. Atlas is a drafting tool, not an operations team. Business can't scale. |
| C | Tiered trust — admin pre-approved, customer-facing draft-and-ask | Admin flows freely. Customer comms require approval until trust is earned. Tiers can be promoted over time. |
| D | Case-by-case — James decides per action | No system. Every action is a judgment call. Exhausting for James. No consistency. Impossible to scale. |

---

## Why This Path

Option C was chosen because it's the only option that scales. Binary trust (full or none) doesn't match reality — some actions are genuinely low-risk and reversible (updating a status field), while others carry permanent reputation consequences (sending an email to a prospect).

The key design principle: the system must make it easy to do the right thing and hard to do the wrong thing. Tier 1 actions are pre-approved because the cost of a mistake is low and the cost of delay is high (if Atlas can't update a record without James, nothing moves). Tier 2 actions require approval because the cost of a mistake is high and the cost of delay is acceptable (a prospect can wait an hour for a reviewed email).

The tiers are not permanent. As Atlas demonstrates reliability, specific Tier 2 actions can be promoted to Tier 1. The first real customer emails will be "monitored automation" — logged and reviewed before scaling trust. This is how trust compounds: prove it works → earn more autonomy → prove it again.

---

## Core Principles Applied

- **Trust is earned in layers** — Automation trust isn't binary. It grows as the system proves itself. (Source: Philosophical Directive 5)
- **Accountability stays with James** — Even when Atlas executes autonomously, responsibility for every customer relationship stays with the founder. (Source: founder interview, Philosophical Directive 5)
- **Principles aren't testable; results are** — If a Tier 2 action consistently produces drafts James approves without changes, that's evidence it can be promoted. The process is measurable. (Source: Philosophical Directive 6)
- **We're asking businesses to trust us with their reputation** — Every automated action represents someone else's business. The bar for autonomous execution must reflect that weight. (Source: Philosophical Directive 9)

---

## Risks Identified

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| James becomes a bottleneck — reviewing everything slows the business | High | Medium | Track approval turnaround. If average exceeds 2 hours, identify candidates for Tier 1 promotion. Continuous reduction of review burden. |
| Tier 1 scope creep — "admin" expands to include things that should be reviewed | Medium | High | Explicit Tier 1 catalog in Trust.md. Any addition requires conscious decision, not drift. |
| A Tier 1 action causes an unexpected customer-visible consequence | Low | High | Log every Tier 1 action. If one causes an issue, review the boundary. The failure teaches us where the line should be. |
| Over-caution keeps too much in Tier 2, slowing momentum | Medium | Medium | Monthly review: what has Atlas drafted that James approved without changes 5+ times? Promote it. |

---

## Expected Outcome

Tier 1 actions execute instantly without James's involvement. Tier 2 actions are drafted and awaiting approval within minutes of trigger. Over time, specific Tier 2 actions graduate to Tier 1 as Atlas demonstrates consistent quality. Within 6 months, 90% of operational tasks are Tier 1. James reviews only genuinely novel or sensitive communications.

---

## Success Criteria

- [ ] Tier 1 actions execute without any James involvement (zero delays)
- [ ] Average Tier 2 approval turnaround under 2 hours
- [ ] James modifies fewer than 20% of Tier 2 drafts before approving (indicating Atlas quality is high)
- [ ] At least 3 Tier 2 action types promoted to Tier 1 within 6 months
- [ ] Zero reputation incidents caused by an automated action
- [ ] Trust.md catalog of Tier 1/Tier 2 actions is current and accurate

---

## Atlas's Challenge (Pre-Decision)

> This decision was established during the interview when James articulated the layered trust concept. The challenge: is "customer-facing" the right boundary? Some internal actions have customer consequences (e.g., changing a prospect's pipeline stage to "Lost" — that affects whether they get follow-up). The boundary might be better drawn as "actions with customer consequences" rather than "customer-facing actions." This nuance should be tracked. If a Tier 1 action ever causes a customer-visible problem, the boundary definition needs refinement.

**James's response:** Accepted the nuance. The principle stands; the boundary definition will be refined through experience.
