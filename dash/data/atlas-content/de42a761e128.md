# James Hardstaff — Founder Interview

**Date:** 2026-06-27
**Interviewer:** Atlas
**Purpose:** Deep-dive interview to understand James's thinking, sales philosophy, decision-making, and vision for Not Forgotten Systems. 20 questions.

---

## Q1: When did Not Forgotten become a real business?

**James:** The moment Travis Krieg Painting became a paying client and the system started producing measurable results. Over a few months, our follow-up process helped recover more than $150,000 worth of work that would likely have been lost. I realised I wasn't just sending follow-up emails — I was solving a revenue problem.

The biggest lesson came later. Travis eventually brought the process in-house by hiring someone. At first, I saw that as losing a client, but it taught me something much more important. Businesses don't necessarily want someone to do the work forever. They want a system they can own — one that's reliable, scalable, and doesn't depend on a single person. That changed the direction of Not Forgotten completely. We stopped thinking like a service business and started building a software platform.

My real customer isn't someone looking for an assistant to chase quotes. It's a business owner who knows they're leaking revenue because follow-up is inconsistent, wants a repeatable sales process, and wants technology that keeps working whether they're in the office, on the tools, or on holiday.

**The product isn't follow-up. The product is confidence that no opportunity is forgotten.**

---

## Q2: What question makes someone reconsider if they don't believe they have a follow-up problem?

**James:** I don't try to convince someone they have a follow-up problem. Instead, I ask questions that make them think about their own business.

*"Once you send a quote, how do you know which ones are still alive?"*

Most business owners pause because they realise they don't actually know. They know how many quotes they send, but they don't know which customers are still considering them, which have chosen a competitor, or which simply got busy and forgot to reply.

Then: *"How many quotes did you send last month, and how many of those did you personally follow up?"*

I'm not trying to catch them out. I'm helping them see the gap between the system they think they have and the one they actually have. Business owners rarely wake up saying "I need better follow-up." They wake up saying "I need more work," "I need more cash flow," or "Why are we quoting so much and winning so little?"

Not Forgotten isn't solving a follow-up problem. It's solving a visibility and revenue problem. Follow-up is simply the mechanism.

---

## Q3: What do you say when they say "I'll think about it"?

**James:** "I'll think about it" is almost never the real objection. It's usually a placeholder because the customer isn't ready to tell you what's actually stopping them. I don't try to overcome the objection on the spot. I try to understand it.

*"Absolutely. Just so I know what to send you, what's the biggest thing you'll be thinking about?"*

That question often uncovers the real concern. If they genuinely need to speak with a partner, they'll tell me. If it's price, they'll tell me. If they don't quite understand how it works, they'll tell me.

Most people don't need more information — they need more confidence. That's why the follow-up is so important. Decisions happen over time, not in a single conversation. The only mistake is treating "I'll think about it" as the end of the conversation. For me, it's simply the beginning of the follow-up journey.

---

## Q4: Are you charging what you're worth, or what the market will bear?

**James:** At the moment, I'm charging what I think the market will bear, not what the product is ultimately worth. The reason is that I'm still earning trust. Not Forgotten is in its early stages. I need paying customers, testimonials, case studies, product feedback, and proof that the system delivers consistently across different businesses. To me, $199 is an entry point, not the destination.

What scares me more isn't charging too little. It's charging too much before I've earned the right. That said, I also know underpricing has its own risks. If I stay at $199 forever, I'll attract customers who buy on price instead of value, and I'll limit my ability to invest in making the product exceptional.

---

## Q5: Could you justify $499/month with what the product delivers today?

**James:** Today, I couldn't confidently justify $499 per month to every prospect based solely on what the product delivers today. Not because I don't think it's worth it, but because the product is still early. That said, I could justify $499 to the right customer — a business that sends 50+ quotes a month, has no structured follow-up process, and regularly loses work because customers go quiet.

What I'm really selling today is consistency. Today the product: ensures every quote is remembered, follows up consistently instead of relying on memory, gives the business owner visibility into their pipeline, saves hours every week, creates a professional customer experience that most small businesses simply don't have.

I don't want customers to buy because I tell them it's worth $499. I want them to use the platform for a month and wonder how they ever operated without it. When that happens, I expect our pricing to increase for new customers. However, I also believe in rewarding loyalty. The businesses that backed Not Forgotten from the beginning should continue to receive their original pricing.

---

## Q6: What's the one thing you're genuinely bad at?

**James:** Building and maintaining detailed systems on my own. I'm an ideas person. I naturally think about strategy, relationships, sales, and where the business should go. What I don't naturally enjoy is documenting every process, maintaining workflows, keeping data perfectly organised, or doing repetitive administrative work. I can do it, but it drains my energy and eventually falls behind.

That weakness has already cost the business. In the early days, Not Forgotten depended almost entirely on me. If I was busy with my full-time job, progress slowed. If I forgot something, the business forgot it too. Processes lived in my head instead of in a system.

That's one of the biggest reasons Atlas exists. I don't want Atlas to replace my strengths. I want it to compensate for my weaknesses. My role is to build relationships, sell the vision, and decide where we're going. Atlas's role is to make sure nothing falls through the cracks as we get there.

---

## Q7: If Atlas makes a mistake, whose fault is it?

**James:** It's my fault. I designed the system, I chose the processes, and I decided what level of autonomy it has. I can't blame the tool for doing exactly what I allowed it to do.

That mindset changes how I build Not Forgotten. I don't want Atlas making important decisions without the right safeguards. Early on, I'd rather Atlas ask for approval than confidently do the wrong thing. Trust isn't built by never making mistakes — it's built by catching mistakes before the customer ever sees them.

The real cost of a mistake isn't having to resend an email or correct a record. It's losing trust. If Atlas sends the wrong message to a customer, the customer won't think "The AI made a mistake." They'll think "Not Forgotten let me down."

A human repeating the same mistake is frustrating. A system repeating the same mistake is unacceptable. Every error becomes a lesson that strengthens the platform.

---

## Q8: You said ask for approval, but you approved auto-creating Quotes. Reconcile.

**James:** There is a difference. The automation I approved isn't making business decisions — it's executing predefined rules. Creating a Client record, generating a Quote record, and updating the Prospect status are administrative tasks that follow rules I've already defined. Atlas isn't deciding whether we should win a customer or how to communicate with them.

Where I draw the line is customer-facing decisions and ambiguous situations. Sending a personalised email? Ask me. Negotiating? Ask me. Handling an unhappy client? Ask me. Interpreting unclear information? Ask me. Anything that could affect a customer relationship? Ask me.

However: creating records, linking data, logging activities, updating statuses based on clear rules, moving information between systems — those are exactly the types of repetitive tasks I want Atlas to automate. Just because the workflow is rule-based doesn't mean it's production-ready. The first phase should be monitored automation. For the first batch of real emails, I want every action logged and reviewed.

**Trust isn't binary. It's earned in layers.**

---

## Q9: If Atlas gets better than you at sales, will you let it?

**James:** If Atlas becomes genuinely better than me at qualifying prospects or running the early stages of sales conversations, then I hope I have the discipline to let it. My goal has never been to prove that I'm the best salesperson. My goal is to build a business that creates value without depending on me.

That doesn't mean I become irrelevant. It means my role evolves. Today I spend a lot of time working in the business. My long-term vision is to work on the business. I don't want to be the bottleneck.

One of the ways I'll measure success: *"If I disappeared for a month, would Not Forgotten continue to serve customers at the same standard?"* If yes, I've built a real business. If no, I've just built a job with better technology.

The only thing I won't hand over is responsibility. Atlas may carry out more of the work, but accountability always stays with me.

---

## Q10: When Atlas makes a decision you disagree with — not a mistake, just different — what do you do?

**James:** It depends, but not on my ego or my opinion. It depends on whether the decision can be measured. If Atlas makes a different judgment call and the outcome is measurable, I'm prepared to let it play out.

However, if the decision affects our values, reputation, or customer trust, then I will override it. If Atlas suggested a follow-up that felt manipulative, dishonest, or inconsistent with how I want Not Forgotten to be known, then the performance doesn't matter. Our reputation is worth more than a short-term increase in conversions.

Atlas and I shouldn't compete to be right. We should be running experiments together. If Atlas has a different idea, I don't want my first reaction to be "That's not how I would do it." I want it to be "Can we test it?"

The only things that aren't up for experimentation are our principles. Results can be tested. Values cannot.

---

## Q11: If Travis is your best proof, why not lead with it every time?

**James:** Proof without relevance doesn't create belief. If I open every conversation by saying "I helped a painting business recover $177,555," most people will think "That's great for Travis." They haven't connected it to their own business yet.

Before someone cares about my solution, they need to recognise their own problem. I'm a firm believer that people buy from people they know, like and trust. That's why I focus on building the relationship first. Once they recognise the gap, that's when the Travis story becomes powerful.

I'm no longer telling them about someone else's success. I'm showing them what's possible in a business that had the same problem they just admitted they have. The cost of leading with my strongest proof is that I risk answering a question they haven't asked yet.

People don't buy because you have an impressive case study. They buy because they recognise themselves in the story. The relationship creates trust. The questions uncover the problem. The case study provides the proof. Only then do I earn the right to sell.

---

## Q12: What's the hardest thing about compressing all that into a 15-second email?

**James:** The hardest part is accepting that I can't build a relationship in one email. A cold email isn't there to make the sale. It's there to earn the next 15 seconds of attention. The first email has one job: make the reader think.

I've also accepted that I can't diagnose someone's business from three sentences. That's why I ask questions instead of making assumptions. If they reply, that's when the real relationship begins. If they don't reply, that's okay too.

The goal of cold outreach isn't to convince everyone. The goal is to identify the businesses that have the problem we solve and begin a genuine conversation with them. A cold email doesn't build trust. It earns the opportunity to start building it.

---

## Q13: What would you have done differently without time constraints? And what constraint turned out to be the right call?

**James:** If time weren't a constraint, I would have been in the market speaking to customers much sooner and much more often. But my biggest constraint has actually forced me into making a better long-term decision.

Because I don't have unlimited time, I couldn't build a service business that depended on me doing everything manually. I had to ask: "How do I build this so it still works while I'm at my full-time job?" That question led me to Atlas. Instead of hiring staff or trying to work another 40 hours a week, I started building systems.

If I'd had unlimited time, there's a good chance I would have taken the easier path — signed more service clients, done more follow-up myself, grown revenue faster in the short term. But I think that would have built the wrong business. It would have created a business that relied on James. Because time has been limited, I've been forced to think like a software founder instead of a service provider.

**My lack of time forced me to build something that doesn't rely on my time.**

---

## Q14: What behaviour or belief makes owners harder to sell to than you expected?

**James:** Most business owners don't believe they have a follow-up problem. They believe they have a sales problem, a marketing problem, or simply that they need more leads. Very few wake up and say "I need a better follow-up system." Most believe they're already following up well enough because they remember the important customers. What they don't see are the dozens of smaller moments where a quote isn't chased, a customer gets busy, or a potential job quietly disappears.

That changed my approach completely. I used to want to explain the platform and show everything it could do. Now I spend far more time asking questions than talking.

I've also learned that many business owners are so busy working in their business that they rarely stop to examine the processes behind it. They don't ignore follow-up because they don't care. They ignore it because they're quoting jobs, managing staff, solving problems, and trying to get through the day. I don't assume they're neglecting their customers. I assume they're overwhelmed. My role isn't to tell them they're doing something wrong. My role is to help them see that there's a simpler, more consistent way.

**I'm not selling software. I'm giving them back headspace. That's what they're really buying.**

---

## Q15: How do you make an invisible benefit feel real enough to pay for?

**James:** I don't think I can sell headspace directly because it's invisible. What I can do is make the causes of stress visible. I can show them: how many quotes are sitting without follow-up, how many customers haven't replied, how many hours they're spending chasing people, how inconsistent their current process is, how many opportunities they can't account for.

Once they see the chaos, they naturally understand the value of removing it. The headspace isn't what I sell. It's what they experience after using the product. Before they become a customer, I have to sell certainty.

I've also learned that I don't need every prospect to believe me. I need enough proof that they believe people like them. That's why customer stories, measurable results and testimonials become so important.

**Not Forgotten isn't selling AI. It isn't selling automation. It isn't even selling follow-up. It's selling certainty.** The certainty that no opportunity is forgotten, no customer is accidentally ignored, and no revenue quietly slips through the cracks because life got busy. Headspace is simply the feeling that certainty creates.

## Q16: How many paying customers do you have right now?

**James:** Zero. That's not because I don't believe in the business. It's because I made a conscious decision to stop trying to scale a business that depended entirely on me. About a month ago, I made the decision to stop chasing customers and start building the foundation for the business I actually want.

I'm investing time now so that, once I begin onboarding customers, the experience is consistent from day one and improves with every customer we add. What's standing between me and my first five customers isn't the product. It's finishing the foundation I'm building and having the confidence to put it in front of real businesses.

Once Atlas reaches the standard I'm aiming for, my focus shifts completely. From that point on, my priority won't be building the system. It will be building the customer base.

---

## Q17: How do you know "building the foundation" isn't just hiding?

**James:** That's a fair challenge. Yes, building can become a sophisticated form of hiding. It's easy to convince yourself that one more feature, one more workflow or one more integration will make launching easier. In reality, perfection doesn't exist.

The difference for me is that I gave myself a defined period to build the foundation. I wasn't trying to avoid customers forever — I was trying to avoid repeating my biggest mistake. I've already proven what happens when everything depends on me. I don't want to build another business where every customer, every follow-up and every decision relies on James.

But that pause can't become permanent. There has to be a point where I stop asking "What else can Atlas do?" and start asking "Who can Atlas help?" Once the core systems are reliable enough to deliver a good customer experience, the priority changes completely. I'm no longer waiting for perfection. I'm waiting for confidence that the customer will receive genuine value.

If I keep building after that point instead of talking to customers, then it won't be building anymore. It'll be hiding.

## Q18: What can't Atlas learn from what you've written down?

**James:** Intuition. When I'm talking to someone, I'm constantly reading things that aren't in the words they're saying. I'm listening to their tone. I'm paying attention to how quickly they reply. I'm noticing whether they're curious, hesitant or just being polite. I'm looking for whether they're asking questions because they're genuinely interested or because they're trying to find a reason not to buy.

I've also learned that not every customer is the right customer. Sometimes I'll walk away from a deal because it doesn't feel right. That's not a decision based on data. It's a decision based on experience and values.

I don't expect Atlas to have that instinct on day one. I expect it to develop good judgment over time by learning from thousands of real interactions, my feedback, and the outcomes of those conversations. I'm not teaching it scripts. I'm teaching it principles.

If Atlas never learns that judgment, then it will always be a very capable assistant. If it does learn that judgment, while always operating within the values and boundaries I've set, then it becomes something much more valuable. It becomes a trusted business partner.

## Q19: Why build an AI instead of just hiring a person?

**James:** The honest answer is that I don't want to build a business that needs more people before it can grow. I've already experienced what happens when a business depends on one person. That person was me.

If I hired someone today, I'd still have to train them, manage them, answer their questions and eventually replace them if they left. Every hour I spend teaching Atlas becomes part of the business forever. It doesn't leave for another job. It doesn't forget what it learned six months ago. Every lesson compounds.

I'm building this business while working full-time and raising a family. Atlas gives me leverage. It allows me to build a business that's bigger than what one person could achieve alone.

I'm not building Atlas because I think AI will replace people. I'm building Atlas because I believe the future belongs to businesses where people and AI work together, each doing what they do best. Atlas isn't my replacement. It's my multiplier. If I teach it well enough, every future employee we hire won't just learn from me. They'll learn from everything Atlas has already learned over the years.

**That's how knowledge becomes part of the company instead of walking out the door every time someone leaves.**

---

## Q20: Three years from now, something goes wrong. What answer are you most afraid of finding?

**James:** The answer I'm most afraid of finding isn't that Atlas made a mistake. It's that I knew there was a weakness in the system and chose to ignore it. If I ever trace a failure back to a decision where I chose speed over quality, growth over trust, or convenience over doing the right thing, that would be the hardest thing to accept.

If a customer calls me three years from now, I don't want my first question to be "Who made the mistake?" I want my first question to be "How did our system allow this to happen?" Blaming people doesn't build better businesses. Improving systems does.

I actually see customer complaints, problems and disappointment differently from most people. To me, they're opportunities — to improve the product, strengthen the system and become a better company. I don't want customers who quietly leave without telling us why. I want customers who challenge us.

The same applies to Atlas. I don't want you to simply agree with everything I say. I want you to challenge my thinking, question my assumptions and point out weaknesses in my ideas. I'm not a dictator who wants to be told I'm right. I'm a founder who's trying to build something great, and that means I need to keep learning.

**Not Forgotten isn't really selling software. We're asking businesses to trust us with part of their reputation. That's a privilege.** And I never want to forget that every email we send, every follow-up we make and every customer interaction represents someone else's business, not just ours. If we ever lose sight of that, we've lost far more than a customer. We've lost the reason the business exists.
