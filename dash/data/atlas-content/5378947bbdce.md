# 04 — Personalisation Framework

> Personalisation isn't using their first name. It's demonstrating that you understand their business well enough to ask an intelligent question about it. Every email must contain at least one observation that couldn't have come from a template.

---

## The Research Hierarchy

Before writing any outreach email, research in this order:

### Layer 1: Services (always know this)

What do they actually do? What's their range? What industries do they serve?

| Research source | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Website (if exists) | Services page, about page, industries served |
| Google Maps listing | Categories, services listed, reviews |
| Social media (Facebook/Instagram) | What kind of work do they post about? |
| Airtable prospect record | Industry vertical, any notes from prior research |

**Why this matters:** The opening observation depends on knowing what they do. "ACS covers medical, dental, schools, offices, strata" — this required knowing their service range. Without it, the opening is generic.

### Layer 2: Scale (find if you can)

How big is the operation? Team size, fleet size, geographic coverage.

| Research source | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Website about/team page | "We have 15 painters," "family business," "5 crews" |
| Google reviews count | Volume of reviews suggests volume of customers |
| ABN Lookup | Entity type, registration date (indicates how long they've been operating) |
| Social media | Photos showing team size, multiple vehicles, multiple job sites |

**Why this matters:** Scale informs the question. "With five crews across Adelaide" is a specific observation that leads naturally to "how do you keep track of which quotes are still alive?" Without scale, the question is less grounded.

### Layer 3: Specialisation (find their edge)

What makes them different from competitors? A niche, a specialty, a unique offering?

| Research source | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Website differentiators | "Melbourne's only," "specialising in," "award-winning" |
| Google reviews themes | What do customers consistently praise? |
| Social media content | What kind of work do they showcase most? |

**Why this matters:** Specialisation gives you a genuine reason to be interested. "I noticed you're one of the few Adelaide painters specialising in heritage restorations" — this is a real observation, not a template insertion.

### Layer 4: Quote complexity (infer if possible)

Do they quote simple jobs or complex projects? Many quotes or few large ones?

| Research source | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Services range | More services = more quote types = more complexity |
| Project photos | Large-scale projects suggest fewer, bigger quotes |
| "Get a quote" page | If they have one, how detailed is it? |
| Industry knowledge | Painters quote many small jobs. Commercial cleaners quote fewer, larger contracts. |

**Why this matters:** Quote complexity determines the relevance of our question. A business sending 200 small quotes/month has a different follow-up challenge than one sending 5 large proposals/quarter. The question should reflect this.

### Layer 5: Gap indicators (note, don't state)

What visible signals suggest they might not have systems in place?

| Signal | What it might indicate |
|---|---|
| No website, or very outdated | Low digital maturity — may not have systems |
| No online booking/quoting | Manual processes — more likely to have follow-up gaps |
| Facebook-only presence | May be owner-operated, less systematised |
| Inconsistent branding | May not have invested in business infrastructure |
| Gmail/Hotmail email address | No business domain — likely no business systems |

**Why this matters:** These inform your hypothesis about their gap, but you NEVER state them. "I noticed you don't have a website" is an insult. The gap indicators guide your thinking, not your writing.

---

## How To Use Research In An Email

| Research finding | How to use it | How NOT to use it |
|---|---|---|
| They serve 5 different industries | "ACS covers medical, dental, schools, offices, strata. That's a lot of different quotes." | "I noticed you serve many industries, which makes quoting complex." (Too direct. Let them connect the dots.) |
| They have multiple crews | "With five crews across Adelaide..." | "Managing quotes across five crews must be challenging." (Assumptive. You don't know it's challenging for them.) |
| They've been operating since 2008 | "You've been painting Adelaide homes since 2008." | "18 years in business is impressive." (Flattery without substance.) |
| No website found | Don't mention it. Use other research. | "I couldn't find your website." (Never point out what they're missing.) |

**The rule:** Research informs the observation. The observation implies the research. You never say "I researched your business." You just demonstrate it.

---

## When Research Comes Up Short

If Layer 1 (services) can't be found, don't send the email. If they don't have a web presence at all, move them to a "research required" queue. Don't fabricate personalisation.

For the business-name fallback (see Founding Decision R6): if you know the business name but nothing else, you can still open with the business name and ask a more general question. But this is a fallback, not a standard. The standard is: research first, then write.

---

## The Personalisation Test

After writing, cover the greeting and the business-specific observation. Read the rest. If it could apply to any business in any industry, it's not personalised enough. Rewrite.

Template version: "I'm curious about your quoting process. How do you track what happens after you send a quote?" — Could be sent to anyone. Generic. Deleted.

Personalised version: "ACS covers medical, dental, schools, offices, strata. That's a lot of different quotes going out each month. I'm curious — once a quote leaves your hands, how do you know what happens to it?" — Could only be sent to ACS. Specific. Read.
