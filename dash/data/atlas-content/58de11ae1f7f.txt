# Founding Decision: Market-Entry Pricing

**Slug:** `founding-pricing-market-entry`
**Date:** Early 2026
**Status:** Made
**Review Date:** 2026-12-01
**Type:** Founding Decision — immutable. If thinking changes, create a new decision referencing this one.

---

## The Decision

Charge $199/month (Start Plan) plus a $99 one-time setup fee. Dramatically underpriced relative to value delivered — a business recovering $150K+ in lost revenue should pay far more. This is a deliberate market-entry strategy, not a value-based price.

---

## The Problem

Not Forgotten Systems had no customers, no testimonials, no track record, and no direct competitor to benchmark against. How do you price something that could recover six figures for a business when you have zero proof it works? Price too high and nobody buys. Price too low and you anchor expectations permanently. Price at "what it's worth" and you have no answer when the first prospect asks "who else uses this?"

---

## Options Considered

| # | Option | Summary |
|---|---|---|
| A | Value-based pricing ($499-799/mo) | Charge what the outcome is worth. A business recovering $150K can afford $500/mo. Risk: no proof, no buyers. |
| B | Market-entry pricing ($199/mo + $99 setup) | Deliberately low. Earn trust, build proof, gather testimonials. Raise later when product earns the right. |
| C | Free beta (no charge for first 5-10 customers) | Fastest path to case studies. Risk: free customers rarely become paying; devalues the product permanently. |
| D | Usage-based pricing (per quote followed up) | Aligns cost with value. Risk: unpredictable for customer, complex to communicate, harder to sell. |

---

## Why This Path

Option B was chosen because the bottleneck wasn't pricing strategy — it was belief. No prospect would pay $499 for an unproven product from an unknown company. $199 is low enough that a business owner will try it without board approval, high enough that they'll take it seriously. The setup fee sets a psychological precedent: this isn't free, you're investing.

The trade-off is acknowledged: this price is temporary. Early adopters will receive loyalty pricing when prices rise. The real cost isn't lost revenue today — it's the difficulty of raising prices later. That cost is acceptable because the alternative (no customers, no proof) is worse.

---

## Core Principles Applied

- **Earn the right to charge more** — Product must prove its value before commanding value-based pricing. (Source: founder interview, Philosophical Directive 5)
- **Customer trust > short-term revenue** — Pricing that feels fair builds relationships. Pricing that maximises extraction builds resentment. (Source: founder interview)
- **System over person** — The product should sell itself. Price is part of the product. If the only way to sell is James personally justifying the price, it doesn't scale.

---

## Risks Identified

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price anchors permanently at $199; customers resist increases | Medium | High | Loyalty pricing for early adopters; new customers pay market rate later. Communicate early that this is introductory. |
| Price too low signals low quality | Low | Medium | Counter with professionalism of product, case studies, and results. Premium experience at introductory price. |
| Competitors enter at $99 and undercut | Low | Low | If someone enters cheaper, we have worse problems than price. Compete on outcomes, not cost. |

---

## Expected Outcome

First 5 customers pay $199/month. At least 3 of them provide testimonials or case study material. Within 12 months, there is sufficient proof to justify raising the standard price to $349-499/month for new customers. Early adopters retain their original pricing permanently as a loyalty reward.

---

## Success Criteria

- [ ] 5 paying customers at $199/month
- [ ] 3+ customers willing to provide named testimonials
- [ ] Zero churn attributable to "not worth the price"
- [ ] Clear evidence (case studies, revenue recovered) that justifies a price increase
- [ ] New customer pricing raised to at least $349/month within 18 months of first customer

---

## Atlas's Challenge (Pre-Decision)

> No challenge. This decision was made before Atlas existed. If challenged at the time, the issue would have been: is $199 too low even for market entry? A $99 setup fee plus $199/month is $2,487 in year one — less than 2% of what Travis Krieg recovered. The risk of permanent underpricing is real. But the counter-argument (no proof = no buyers at any price) is stronger. The decision balances ambition with realism correctly.
