# 06 — Common Mistakes

> Every mistake is a system improvement opportunity. This catalogue exists so the same mistake is never made twice. If you discover a new mistake, add it here immediately.

---

## Mistake 1: Explaining the product in the first email

**What happened:** The first draft of the ACS Commercial Cleaning email described what Not Forgotten does and pitched the solution. It was overly explanatory — it tried to do the relationship-building AND the product education in one email.

**Why it was wrong:** The first email's only job is to make the reader think. You can't build a relationship, diagnose a business, AND pitch a product in 100 words. Trying to do all three means doing none of them well.

**What we learned:** The first email should contain zero product information. Not a hint. Not a "we help businesses like yours." Nothing. The product appears only after the prospect has engaged with the question and recognised their own gap.

**The fix:** Cut everything that described the product. The final version: one observation, one question, one reason. 87 words. Zero product.

**Status:** Principle encoded. All future first emails audited for product mentions before sending.

## Mistake 2: Leading with the case study

**What almost happened:** In early planning, the instinct was to open with the Travis Krieg story — "$177,555 recovered for an Adelaide painter." It's our strongest proof. It's tempting to lead with it.

**Why it's wrong:** Proof without relevance doesn't create belief. A commercial cleaner reading about a painter's success thinks "that's impressive, but my business is different." The case study only becomes powerful after the prospect has recognised their own gap.

**What we learned:** Sequence matters more than content. Travis is the bridge between problem recognition and confidence to act — not the opener. (See Founding Decision R7.)

**The fix:** Travis appears only after the prospect has engaged with a question and demonstrated recognition of their own gap. Never in a first email.

**Status:** Principle encoded in Founding Decision R7.

---

## Mistake 3: Jargon and feature language

**What almost happened:** Draft language sometimes drifted into feature descriptions — "automated follow-up," "CRM," "quote tracking." These are internal terms. Prospects don't think in features.

**Why it's wrong:** Features describe what the product does. Prospects care about what the product changes. "Automated follow-up" is a feature. "You never have to wonder if a quote was seen" is an outcome. The first sells software. The second sells certainty.

**What we learned:** Audit every draft for feature language. If the words "automation," "follow-up," "CRM," "platform," or "software" appear in a first email, they don't belong there.

**The fix:** Replace feature language with outcome language. Not "we automate follow-up" but "you always know where every quote stands."

**Status:** Encoded in writing style guide (02-writing-style.md).

---

## Mistake 4: Too many questions

**What almost happened:** Early drafts sometimes included multiple questions — one about their process, one about their volume, one about their pain points. Multi-question emails feel like interrogations.

**Why it's wrong:** One question focuses attention. Multiple questions scatter it. The reader doesn't know which to answer, so they answer none.

**What we learned:** One email, one question. That's it. If you have three good questions, that's three emails — and you've only earned the right to send the first one.

**The fix:** After writing, count the question marks. If there's more than one (excluding the sign-off), cut until there's one.

**Status:** Encoded in core principles and writing style guide.

---

## Mistake 5: Asking for too much

**What almost happened:** Drafts sometimes included "would you be open to a quick chat?" or "let me know if you'd like to see how it works." These are asks for a meeting or demo in the first email.

**Why it's wrong:** The first email hasn't earned a meeting. The only thing it's earned (if it's good) is a reply. Asking for a call is skipping three steps on the commitment ladder.

**What we learned:** The first email's only conversion metric is "did they reply?" Everything else — calls, demos, trials — comes after multiple genuine interactions.

**The fix:** The ask is implicit in the question. If the question is good enough, a reply is natural. No explicit "would you like to chat" language.

**Status:** Encoded in psychological principles (minimal ask).

---

## Mistake 6: Template-like language

**What almost happened:** Phrases like "I hope this finds you well," "I wanted to reach out," and "I was wondering if" crept into early drafts. These are filler. They signal "this is a template" before the reader has reached the first real sentence.

**Why it's wrong:** Filler words cost attention. The first sentence is the most valuable real estate in the email. Wasting it on "I hope this finds you well" tells the reader: this is going to be like every other cold email you delete.

**What we learned:** The first sentence should be specific to their business. No warm-up. No throat-clearing. Start with them.

**The fix:** If the email starts with a phrase that could appear in any email to anyone, delete it and start with the next sentence.

**Status:** Encoded in writing style guide (the opening).

---

## Mistake 7: Making it about us

**What almost happened:** "My name is Atlas..." / "Not Forgotten Systems helps..." / "We specialise in..." — self-introductions and company descriptions in the opening.

**Why it's wrong:** The email should be about the reader's business, not ours. Every "we" statement shifts focus away from them. The first time they learn who we are should be in the signature, after we've demonstrated we understand their business.

**What we learned:** Count the pronouns. If "we" or "our" appears in the body of a first email, something is wrong. The email is about them.

**The fix:** "We" and "our" are forbidden in first email bodies. They can appear in the "reason I ask" sentence only if necessary ("I've been working with service businesses on exactly this" — "I," not "we").

**Status:** Encoded in writing style guide and "What We NEVER Do."

---

## Mistake 8: Using em dashes

**What happened:** Em dashes appeared in drafts — they're a natural writing pattern for AI-generated text. But they read as AI-generated.

**Why it's wrong:** Em dashes are uncommon in casual business email. Their presence signals "this was written by a language model." Trust is built on feeling human. Anything that breaks that feeling damages trust.

**What we learned:** Audit every outbound message for em dashes before sending. Replace with commas, colons, or full stops.

**The fix:** Encoded as a rule in SOUL.md ("Outbound writing: Never use em dashes") and writing style guide.

**Status:** Permanent rule. Applied to all outbound writing, not just outreach.

---

## How To Use This File

Before sending any outreach email, scan this list. Has the draft made any of these mistakes? If yes, fix before sending. If a new mistake is discovered (a reply that went badly, a pattern that emerged), add it here immediately with the same structure: what happened, why it was wrong, what we learned, the fix, status.
