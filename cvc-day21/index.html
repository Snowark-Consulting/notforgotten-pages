The Treatment group (with woven context sentence) produced zero replies of any kind. Open rates were lower in the Treatment group (50% vs 70%). Adding a context sentence may have actually reduced engagement.

2. What We Learned

Patterns

  • No reply is itself a strong signal. 40 painters, 4 touchpoints each (~160 emails), 21 days — zero meaningful replies. This is not a sample-size problem. Cold email to painters is a low-reply channel regardless of copy variations.
  • Open rates were lower with context. Treatment group showed 50% open rate vs Control's 70%. Average opens: 1.9 vs 2.8. The context sentence may have made emails feel longer or more marketing-like, reducing open behaviour.
  • Unsubscribe was immediate. The sole reply (Distinct Painters) unsubscribed on July 13 — before Email 1 was sent on July 14. This was a pre-existing disengagement, not a reaction to the experiment.

What This Doesn't Mean

  • It does NOT mean context is harmful. The sample is too small to conclude directionality with confidence. But it provides zero evidence that context helps.
  • It does NOT mean cold email to painters is dead. The July campaigns had 0/65 Email 1 replies. This is consistent with a channel where engagement comes through sustained presence, not first-contact conversion.
  • It does NOT invalidate the broader outreach philosophy. FF1 is testing a fundamentally different approach (founder observations, not curiosity prompts).

3. What Should Change

  1. Do not use the woven context sentence in future outreach. Evidence: zero benefit in Treatment group, lower open rates. The sentence "I've been asking painting business owners about this lately..." added length without adding value.
  2. Do not run curiosity-only painter campaigns again. Evidence: 0/40 meaningful replies across both groups, plus 0/65 July Email 1 replies. The curiosity prompt format has been tested to exhaustion with no result.
  3. FF1's founder-to-founder approach is the correct pivot. Evidence: curiosity-based approaches produced zero meaningful engagement across 105 painter prospects. FF1 tests a fundamentally different mechanism — James sharing observations as a peer, not prompting curiosity as a stranger.

4. What Should Remain Exactly the Same

  • Experiment methodology. The CvC design (random assignment, stratified, pre-registered hypothesis, taxonomy-based classification) is sound. The result was negative, but the process produced a clear answer. This methodology should be reused for future experiments.
  • Reply taxonomy (T0-T5). Worked perfectly for this analysis — the distinction between T1 dismissal and T3+ meaningful reply prevented over-interpreting the one reply.
  • 21-day checkpoint window. Sufficient time for all 4 touchpoints to complete. No late replies emerged.

5. Implications for FF1

The CvC result strengthens the case for FF1's approach in two ways:

  1. It eliminates the curiosity-prompt format as a viable strategy. We now have evidence across 105 painter prospects that "Have you noticed X? I'm curious how you handle it" does not generate meaningful replies. This is no longer a hypothesis — it's a finding.
  2. It makes FF1 the cleanest test possible. FF1's "Something I've noticed" approach — where James shares a genuine observation about a specific business, without asking a curiosity question — is structurally different from everything we've tested. There's no confound from the CvC format. FF1 is a genuinely new variable.
Bottom line: CvC closed the book on curiosity-based cold outreach to painters. FF1 opens a new chapter with a fundamentally different mechanism. The CvC result makes FF1 more important, not less — it's now the active experiment that matters.
